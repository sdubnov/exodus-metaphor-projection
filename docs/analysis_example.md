# Analysis Example: Historical Exodus Corpus Figures

This note records what the hosted historical-corpus figures are meant to show. It can be used while revising the paper and as a small GitHub tutorial.

The figures are generated by the reference implementation from the local Exodus research corpus. They are not exact reproductions of the submitted paper figures.

The limited sample data included in this GitHub repository is for testing the code path only. Running the public sample corpus will not reproduce the hosted figures below.

## How To Generate Comparable Figures

The hosted figures were generated from local corpus manifests that point to the Exodus research texts. Those manifests are not committed because the underlying corpus includes texts that may not be redistributable.

For the exact local commands and corpus-file convention used for these figures, see [historical_corpus_workflow.md](historical_corpus_workflow.md).

To generate comparable figures with your own local corpus, create a manifest and run:

```bash
python scripts/run_metaphor_projection_demo.py \
  --anchors data/anchors/berlin_liberty_anchor_pairs.csv \
  --manifest path/to/my_manifest.json \
  --output outputs/figures/my_metaphor_projection.html \
  --seed 42 \
  --hide-hover-text
```

## What The Graphs Represent

Each point is a text chunk or paragraph. The color indicates the source text. In private/local runs, hovering over a point can reveal the corresponding passage. In the hosted GitHub Pages figures, passage text is hidden to avoid redistributing the local research corpus.

The 3D unguided embedding baseline shows the local historical corpus embedded with the sentence-transformer model and reduced to three principal components with PCA. It is useful as a broad, model-internal view of distributional proximity, but it is not anchor-guided and should not be interpreted as the main metaphor projection.

The two metaphor projection plots show selected Exodus-corpus texts projected through a learned two-dimensional contrastive space trained from manually curated liberty-related anchor pairs. These plots ask whether passages become comparable under a shared metaphorical vocabulary of bondage, deliverance, law, covenant, trial, and self-rule.

## Interpretation For Paper Revision

The projections illustrate the central methodological claim of the study: manually curated metaphor anchors can define an interpretive space for comparing passages across source texts. The anchor-guided projection reorganizes passages according to a curated metaphorical vocabulary of bondage, deliverance, law, covenant, trial, and self-rule.

In a local historical-corpus run, Exodus and Surah Al-Qasas can be inspected for overlapping structures of divine authority, oppression, trial, and liberation. Modern political texts can be inspected for different transformations of liberty metaphors: Douglass and King around oppression, moral struggle, and delayed justice; Herzl around civic construction, collective destiny, and institutional realization.

These visualizations are exploratory and interpretive rather than conclusive. They identify candidate affinities for close reading and motivate future robustness studies over anchor choice, corpus composition, random seed, and projection stability.

## Possible Captions

### 3D Unguided Embedding Baseline

Paragraphs from the local historical Exodus corpus are embedded with a sentence-transformer model and projected into three dimensions with PCA. The plot provides an exploratory view of broad distributional proximity among texts before applying the anchor-guided metaphor projection. It is included as a diagnostic and interpretive baseline rather than as a formal validation result.

### Religious Metaphor Projection

Paragraphs from Exodus and Surah Al-Qasas are projected into a learned metaphor space trained from curated liberty-related anchor pairs. The plot is intended as an exploratory map of candidate cross-traditional affinities around divine authority, oppression, trial, deliverance, and the formation of a community under law.

### Modern Political Metaphor Projection

Paragraphs from Herzl, Martin Luther King Jr., and Frederick Douglass are projected into the same anchor-guided metaphor space. The visualization can be read as an exploratory comparison of different modern transformations of Exodus-like liberty metaphors, including oppression, moral struggle, delayed justice, civic construction, and collective realization.
